Colab and your Hugging Face token

Colab gives you a Linux machine with a GPU for as long as your session lasts. Choose Runtime → Change runtime type → T4 GPU, and store your token under the key-shaped Secrets icon rather than in a cell; read it with from google.colab import userdata; userdata.get("HF_TOKEN") and log in with huggingface_hub.login. Gated models fail with an authorization error until you've accepted their license on the model's page.

Colab sessions are disposable

When the runtime disconnects, installed packages, downloaded models and files in /content are gone. Save outputs to Google Drive (mount it with drive.mount) or push them to the Hugging Face Hub, and expect to re-run setup cells after a break.

Pipelines: the high-level API

A pipeline bundles a default model, its tokenizer and the pre- and post-processing for a task. It's the quickest way to see what an open model can do.

The same pattern covers named-entity recognition, question answering, translation, zero-shot classification, text generation, speech recognition and more; the Diffusers library extends it to images. The trade-off is control: you get the task's default model and settings unless you pass your own with model=.

Tokenizers and chat templates

Each model family has its own tokenizer, with its own vocabulary and special tokens that mark where the system prompt, user turns and assistant turns start and stop. A chat model was trained on conversations wrapped in those markers, so yours must be wrapped the same way. The tokenizer's chat template does that for you.

Print the template for two or three model families side by side and you'll see why mixing up tokenizers or skipping the template produces garbage: the model is being shown a format it never learned.

Models: loading, quantizing, generating

At 16-bit precision each parameter takes two bytes, so an 8-billion-parameter model needs roughly 16 GB of GPU memory just to load, more than a T4 offers. Quantization stores weights at lower precision. Loading in 4-bit cuts memory to roughly a quarter, at a small cost in quality, which is what makes these models fit on a cheap GPU.

Printing model shows its architecture: an embedding layer, a stack of identical decoder layers with attention and feed-forward blocks, and a final layer that scores every token in the vocabulary. You'll meet these layer names again in Week 7, when QLoRA attaches small trainable adapters to some of them.

What "generation" actually is

A model scores every possible next token, one is chosen, it's appended, and the process repeats. You can watch this through an API: ask for logprobs=True, top_logprobs=3 and each step of the reply comes back with the three most likely candidates and their log-probabilities. Plot them as a branching tree and the abstraction becomes concrete. Settings such as temperature change how the choice is made.

Meeting minutes: two models in a row

The project chains two open models. A speech-to-text model (for example one from OpenAI's open Whisper family, through pipeline("automatic-speech-recognition")) transcribes the audio; then a chat model turns the transcript into minutes with a fixed structure. It's the same two-step shape as Week 1's brochure writer, now running entirely on models you host.

Takeaways

  • Colab rents you a GPU; keep tokens in Secrets and outputs in Drive or the Hub.
  • pipeline is the fast path; tokenizers and models are the controllable path.
  • Chat models need their own chat template; let the tokenizer apply it.
  • Quantization to 4-bit is what makes billion-parameter models fit on a small GPU.

Check your understanding

Loading a Llama model fails with an authorization error even though your token is set. Why?

Gated repositories require you to accept the model's terms on its page before your token can download it.

Roughly how much memory do the weights of an 8B-parameter model need in 4-bit?

8 billion parameters × half a byte is about 4 GB, plus overhead. At 16-bit it would be about 16 GB.

What does apply_chat_template do?

Each model family has its own conversation format; the template produces it, ending where the reply should begin.
