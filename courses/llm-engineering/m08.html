Why not just train the whole model?

Full fine-tuning updates every one of the model's parameters. For 3 billion parameters, that means holding the weights, their gradients and the optimizer's state in GPU memory at once, which is many times the size of the model and far beyond a single affordable GPU. Two ideas fix this.

LoRA: train a small add-on, not the model

Low-Rank Adaptation freezes the original weights and, beside chosen layers, adds two small matrices whose product acts as a correction to that layer. Only those small matrices are trained. The result is an adapter of a few megabytes to tens of megabytes that you load on top of the base model.

QLoRA: and keep the frozen model in 4-bit

Because the base weights are frozen, they can be stored quantized, in 4-bit as you did in Week 3, while the small adapters train at higher precision. That's QLoRA, and it's what lets a single T4 or similar GPU fine-tune a model of this size.

The hyperparameters that matter

SettingWhat it controlsRule of thumb
Rank rWidth of the adapter matrices, and so how much it can learnStart small (8–32); raise it if the model underfits
AlphaHow strongly the adapter's correction is scaledCommonly set to about twice the rank
Target modulesWhich layers get adaptersUsually the attention projections; adding the MLP layers increases capacity and cost
DropoutRandomly disables parts of the adapter in training, to reduce overfittingAround 0.1
Learning rateSize of each updateToo high diverges, too low barely moves; use a warmup and decay schedule
Epochs and batch sizeHow many passes over the data, and how many examples per stepWatch the loss; more data usually beats more epochs

Treat these numbers as a starting point for your own runs and adjust from what the loss and evaluation tell you.

Teach the format, not just the facts

Each training example is a prompt that ends exactly where the answer begins, followed by the price. That trains the model to produce a number in the expected place, which makes its output easy to parse. Evaluate the base model before training and you'll see how poorly it does; the first leaderboard entry for this week confirms it: the 4-bit base model is worse than guessing the average.

Base model, not instruct

Fine-tune the base Llama 3.2 3B rather than a chat-tuned version. For a narrow task with a fixed output format, a base model trained on your prompt format doesn't need the chat template or conversational habits, and the format you train is the format you get.

Training, then the moment of truth

Training takes hours on the full dataset. Watch the training loss fall and level off, keep checkpoints on the Hub so a Colab disconnect doesn't lose your work, and then evaluate with the very same Tester from Week 6. Same test, same sample: the only thing that changed is the model.

Two lessons sit in that chart. Data volume mattered enormously: the same recipe on the lite dataset lands mid-table, while the full dataset takes first place. And specialization beats size for a narrow task: a 3-billion-parameter model, trained on the right data, outperforms frontier models that are vastly larger.

Evaluating on what you trained on

If test items leak into training, the numbers will look wonderful and mean nothing. Keep the test split separate from the start (Week 6 does), and don't tune hyperparameters against the final test set; hold out a validation set for that.

Takeaways

  • LoRA trains small adapter matrices instead of the whole model; QLoRA keeps the frozen base in 4-bit, so one modest GPU is enough.
  • Rank, alpha, target modules, learning rate and data volume are the levers that matter most.
  • Train on prompts in exactly the format you'll use at inference time.
  • Evaluate with the unchanged test harness; here, a fine-tuned 3B model beat every frontier model on this task.

Check your understanding

In QLoRA, which parameters are updated during training?

The quantized base stays frozen. Training only touches the adapters, which is why memory needs are so low.

The same QLoRA recipe on the lite dataset lands mid-table; on the full dataset it takes first place. What does that suggest?

The recipe was the same; the data volume changed. That's usually the strongest lever in fine-tuning.

Why does each training example end with the prompt's answer marker followed by just the price?

You get the format you train. A fixed, minimal answer format makes outputs easy to parse and evaluate.
